Gardening in Winter

By Clara O. • Memoir

People often ask why I spend so much time in the garden during January. "There is nothing out there," they say, gesturing to the frost-bitten lawn and the skeletal branches of the hydrangeas. "Everything is dead."

They are wrong, of course. Winter is not death; it is a very deep sleep. It is the only time the earth gets to rest. If you kneel down—ignoring the damp that seeps into your jeans—and scrape away the layer of wet leaves near the hellebores, you can see it. The tiny, pale green knuckles of new life pushing up, just waiting for the signal.

I garden in winter not to harvest, but to listen. In summer, the garden is a chaotic orchestra of bees, lawnmowers, and growing things shouting for water. In winter, the garden whispers. It tells you where the wind hits the hardest. It shows you the true structure of the trees without the vanity of their leaves.

Yesterday, I planted tulip bulbs in soil that was nearly frozen. It felt like an act of faith. I am burying these brown, papery things in the cold dark, trusting that in four months, they will remember how to be beautiful. Perhaps that is why I am out here. To remind myself that even in the coldest times, the work of living continues underground.
